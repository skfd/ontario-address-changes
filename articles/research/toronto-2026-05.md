## 1b. Chloe Cooley — the person

**Searched:** `Chloe Cooley 1793 Upper Canada Act to Limit Slavery`; then read four
independent authorities in full.

**Sources used:**
- The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Chloe Cooley", article by **Natasha Henry-Dixon**, published
  2013-10-30, last edited **2025-12-31** — the fullest and most current scholarly account:
  https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/chloe-cooley-and-the-act-to-limit-slavery-in-upper-canada
- Dictionary of Canadian Biography, "COOLEY, CHLOE":
  https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/cooley_chloe_4E.html
- Ontario Heritage Trust provincial plaque background paper (plaque unveiled at Queenston
  Heights **August 23, 2007**; researched by Colin McCullough, York University, with Adrienne
  Shadd; © 2007): https://www.heritagetrust.on.ca/provincial-plaque-program/provincial-plaque-background-papers/chloe-cooley
- Parks Canada, Chloe Cooley National Historic Person:
  https://parks.canada.ca/culture/designation/personnage-person/chloe-cooley
- Parks Canada news release, designation announced 2022-04:
  https://www.canada.ca/en/parks-canada/news/2022/04/government-of-canada-recognizes-chloe-cooley-as-a-person-of-national-historic-significance.html

**FACT — what happened.** On **14 March 1793**, Chloe Cooley, a Black woman enslaved in
**Queenston, Upper Canada**, was tied up with rope, forced into a boat and taken across the
**Niagara River** to be sold in New York State. She resisted violently and screamed. (All four
sources agree on the date and the act.)

**FACT — the enslaver's name is inconsistent across sources.** The Canadian Encyclopedia,
the Dictionary of Canadian Biography and Parks Canada all name **Sergeant Adam Vrooman**, a
United Empire Loyalist of Queenston. The **Ontario Heritage Trust 2007 background paper says
"William Vrooman"** — and the **City of Toronto's own naming report repeats "William Vrooman"**.
Per Canadian Encyclopedia and Parks Canada, Vrooman was assisted by **his brother Isaac Vrooman**
and one of the sons of Loyalist McGregory Van Every. **Use "Adam Vrooman"**; the City report is
wrong on this point, apparently following the older plaque text.

**FACT — the witnesses.** **Peter Martin**, a Black Loyalist (Canadian Encyclopedia: formerly
enslaved by John Butler; Ontario Heritage Trust: a free Black and former soldier in Butler's
Rangers) and **William Grisley**, a white labourer. They reported it to the Executive Council
of Upper Canada, meeting at Newark (now Niagara-on-the-Lake), on **21 March 1793** (OHT).
The two sources differ on the sequence: OHT says Grisley witnessed it and informed Martin;
the Canadian Encyclopedia says Cooley's scream alerted Martin, and Grisley — who was on the
boat but did not help restrain her — gave the detailed account.

**FACT — what happened to Vrooman.** Attorney General **John White** was directed to prosecute
Vrooman for disturbing the peace. Vrooman replied by petition on **18 April 1793** pleading
ignorance of any law against selling his property. The charges were dropped: enslaved people
were chattel with no legal standing. (Canadian Encyclopedia; OHT; DCB.)

**FACT — the legislation.** Attorney General White introduced an abolition bill to the House of
Assembly on **19 June 1793**. It met opposition from assembly members who themselves enslaved
people (OHT: at least six of the 16 assembly members owned slaves). A compromise version,
**"An Act to Prevent the further Introduction of Slaves and to limit the Term of Contracts for
Servitude"**, received Royal Assent on **9 July 1793**.

**FACT — what the Act did and did not do (this is the part usually got wrong).** Canadian
Encyclopedia, verbatim: *"The Act did not free any enslaved persons in the province. At the
outset, it confirmed and validated the institution of slavery. The Act prohibited the
importation of enslaved persons into Upper Canada but did not outlaw the sale of slaves within
the province or across the border into the United States."* Children born to enslaved mothers
were to be freed at age 25; their children were born free. Slavery in the British Empire ended
with the Slavery Abolition Act, effective **1 August 1834**.

**FACT — her fate is unknown.** DCB: *"Nothing is known of what became of Cooley afterwards."*
Canadian Encyclopedia: *"Little is known about Chloe Cooley's early life or where she went
after March 1793."*

**FACT — earlier life.** Canadian Encyclopedia only: Cooley was first enslaved by **Benjamin
Hardison** of Bertie Township (now Fort Erie), then sold to Vrooman some time before 1793.
Vrooman also enslaved a Black man named **Tom**, sold to Adam Krysler in 1792.

**FACT — commemoration.** Designated a **person of national historic significance in 2022**
(Parks Canada). Canada Post commemorative stamp for **Black History Month 2023** (Canadian
Encyclopedia; Canada Post: https://www.canadapost-postescanada.ca/blogs/personal/perspectives/chloe-cooley-black-history-month/).
Ontario Heritage Trust provincial plaque at Queenston Heights, unveiled 23 August 2007.
Historica Canada Heritage Minute: https://www.historicacanada.ca/en/productions/minutes/chloe-cooley
CBC on the Heritage Minute: https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/heritage-minute-on-black-slavery-chloe-cooley-1.6402849

**Commonly repeated claims to avoid or handle carefully:**
1. **"Simcoe abolished slavery in Upper Canada in 1793."** Not supported. The 1793 Act freed
   nobody already enslaved and, per the Canadian Encyclopedia, *confirmed and validated* the
   institution. Simcoe *wanted* abolition; the Assembly forced a compromise.
2. **"William Vrooman"** — the name in the 2007 OHT plaque paper and in the City's own report.
   Current scholarship (Henry-Dixon, DCB, Parks Canada) says **Adam** Vrooman.
3. **"First legislation in the British Empire to limit slavery."** The wording varies by source:
   OHT says "first piece of legislation in the British Empire to limit slavery"; the Canadian
   Encyclopedia says "the first legislation in the British **colonies** to restrict the **slave
   trade**"; Parks Canada says "the first legislation of its kind in the British Empire." Prefer
   the Canadian Encyclopedia's narrower phrasing.
4. **Anything about what became of Chloe Cooley after 14 March 1793.** Both DCB and the
   Canadian Encyclopedia say it is unknown. Do not assert she was sold to a named buyer or died
   in any particular place.
5. **"She escaped" / "she crossed the river to freedom."** Not supported — she was forcibly
   taken across the river and sold. (A WebFetch summariser hallucinated exactly this error
   mid-research; it is a plausible-sounding trap.)

**Confidence: confirmed.**

## 2. BMO Field → "Toronto Stadium, BMO Field" (170 Princes' Blvd, 2026-05-12)

**Searched:** `BMO Field renamed "Toronto Stadium" FIFA World Cup 2026`; `Toronto Stadium BMO
Field FIFA clean venue policy sponsor name Toronto Ontario`; `"BMO Field" name back August 2026
after World Cup Toronto FC`; `City of Toronto FIFA World Cup 26 Toronto Stadium six matches`;
plus post-tournament reversion searches.

**FACT — the rename and the reason.** BMO Field hosted 2026 FIFA World Cup matches and was
temporarily renamed **"Toronto Stadium"** in accordance with FIFA's policy on corporate-sponsored
venue names. The same thing happened for the 2014 FIFA U-20 Women's World Cup ("National Soccer
Stadium"). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMO_Field
NOW Toronto (2026-06-25) states the change complied with FIFA rules prohibiting brand sponsors
with no affiliation to the tournament:
https://nowtoronto.com/news/from-bmo-field-to-toronto-stadium-whats-next-for-the-sports-venue-after-fifa/
CP24 (2026-07-03) attributes it to "FIFA's strict marketing rules":
https://www.cp24.com/local/toronto/2026/07/03/toronto-stadium-is-returning-to-bmo-field-roots-again-as-temporary-seating-gets-removed/

**FACT — the City used the dual form itself.** The City of Toronto news release writes it as
**"Toronto Stadium (BMO Field)"** — i.e. exactly the pattern the address file adopted:
https://www.toronto.ca/news/goal-city-of-toronto-scores-six-matches-as-a-fifa-world-cup-26-host-city/
The venue's own site carries a City/MLSE item titled "…enhancements to BMO Field (Toronto
Stadium)…" and lists the address as **170 Princes' Boulevard, Toronto, ON M6K 3C3** — the exact
address in the dataset: https://www.bmofield.com/events/fifa26

**FACT — the matches.** Six matches in Toronto: five group-stage on **June 12, 17, 20, 23 and
26, 2026**, plus one **Round of 32 match on July 2, 2026**. The June 12 match was the first
men's World Cup match on Canadian soil and Canada's opener. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMO_Field ,
https://www.toronto.ca/news/goal-city-of-toronto-scores-six-matches-as-a-fifa-world-cup-26-host-city/
The City states it hosted six matches and the FIFA Fan Festival from **June 11 to July 19**:
https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/festivals-events/fifa-world-cup-26/
(Individual fixture matchups come from Wikipedia only — lower confidence than the dates.)

**FACT + INFERENCE — reversion.** CP24, 2026-07-03, reported the Toronto Stadium name returning
to BMO Field: FIFA's exclusive-use period ended **July 9**, FIFA branding removed by **July 20**,
temporary seating removal targeted for **July 31**. As of 2026-08-28 the venue's own site is
branded "BMO Field" throughout, with "Toronto Stadium" surviving only in one archived document
title. **INFERENCE:** the reversion happened. No single post-tournament article stating the
completed reversion was found in two searches.

**Date gap.** The place-name change landed **2026-05-12**, exactly **31 days** before the first
Toronto match (2026-06-12) — recorded a month ahead of the tournament, not during it.

**Nothing found:** FIFA.com's host-city and Toronto Stadium pages appeared in search results but
returned empty bodies on fetch — no FIFA.com citation is available. Also dropped for lack of a
citable URL: a total-attendance figure and a Toronto FC return date that appeared only in search
summaries.

**Confidence: confirmed** (rename, reason, match dates, reversion).
Follow-up worth doing: check whether the file reverts 170 Princes' Blvd to "CNE BMO Field" in
the July or August 2026 snapshots — that would be a nice bookend for the newsletter.
