# The address file is five years behind Secord Avenue

On 2 April 2026, eleven addresses came off Toronto's address file at once: 10, 12, 14, 16
and 18 Secord Avenue, then 40, 42, 44, 46, 48 and 50. All on the even side of one short
street in East York, north of Danforth near Main Street Station. All on file since our
first snapshot in April 2025.

The buildings had been gone for years.

Those eleven are the townhouse units covered by a rental-demolition permit whose issue
date the City publishes: **25 March 2021**. They were cleared to build Bela Square, the
DBS Developments project on the block — whose first phase at 94 Eastdale Avenue was
finished in May 2024 and whose 35-storey tower at 100 Eastdale began occupancy in March
2025. The address file caught up on 2 April 2026, five years after the permit.

## The rest of the street is the proof

If that were the whole story it would be a curiosity. What makes it checkable is the
half of the street that *didn't* change.

A December 2024 staff report quotes the original 2013 council approval verbatim — a
permit to demolish 22 rental townhouses at "90 Eastdale Avenue (Units 92-108 and 101A)
and 2 Secord Avenue (**Units 8-18 and 40-50**)". That is, number for number, the set that
left the file.

The same report describes what comes next: a further 31 units "subject to secondary
addresses inclusive of **20-38 Secord Avenue**". Those are the evens still standing on
the file today — and the City's rental-demolition register shows their permit has **not
been issued**, as of its 3 August 2026 refresh.

So the address file's Secord Avenue is not a snapshot of the street. It is a map of which
demolition permits have been issued. Phase one: gone. Phase two: approved, not permitted,
still addressed.

One number spoils the symmetry, and it should be said: **8 Secord Avenue is inside the
2013 permit range and is still on the file.** No source explains that. The City's own
records also disagree with themselves on when Council approved the thing — the 2024
report cites an item from July 2013, while the rental-demolition register records the
approval as 23 July 2018 for the same file. Both are City sources. I can't reconcile them
and haven't tried to.

## How far behind is it, exactly?

Once you start checking April's changes against the City's own permit and committee
records, the lag turns out to vary by more than an order of magnitude.

| What happened | When | Address appears | Lag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 155 Poyntz Ave severance approved | 14 Aug 2025 | Apr 2026 | ~8 months |
| 407 Arlington laneway-suite permit issued | 12 May 2025 | 27 Apr 2026 | ~11½ months |
| 2650 St Clair construction permits issued | 16–22 Jan 2026 | 13 Apr 2026 | ~3 months |
| 623 Broadview garden-suite permit issued | 19 Jan 2026 | 27 Apr 2026 | ~3 months |
| 152 Pinegrove Ave severance approved | 11 Feb 2026 | Apr 2026 | ~2 months |
| Secord Ave demolition permit issued | 25 Mar 2021 | 2 Apr 2026 | ~5 years |

There is no single answer to "how current is Toronto's address data". It depends entirely
on which event you think an address is recording.

## Two streets named for a leather factory

**Everwood Gardens** and **Commerce Trail** appear in the file for the first time on 13
April, with three and two addresses. They are 20 metres apart, and they are both *private*
streets inside one development — 2650 St Clair Avenue West, the former Danier Leather
factory, where Dunpar Homes is building 98 back-to-back townhouses in six blocks.

The City report proposing the names is dated **30 January 2026** and includes the
developer's rationale, which is worth quoting because street names are rarely explained
at all:

> Commerce Trail "reflects the historical economic significance of the St. Clair Avenue
> West area … Once part of the city's thriving Stockyards District, the neighbourhood
> supported meatpacking, rail-based logistics, and manufacturing industries"

Everwood Gardens, less grandly, "combines 'ever,' suggesting longevity and continuity,
with 'wood,' evoking trees, greenery, and a connection to nature." The names were
circulated to police, fire and paramedic services, who had no objection. Signage: an
estimated $1,200, payable by the applicant.

All five addresses arrived as **reserved** — assigned, not yet in service — which is what
you would expect ten weeks after a naming report and three months after the construction
permits were issued.

The third street new to the file that month, **Sandown Lane** in Scarborough, is not new
at all: it's an existing public laneway behind the north side of Kingston Road in
Cliffside, described in a walking blog back in 2021. Nothing in the City's development,
committee or permit records touches 170 or 172 Sandown Lane. What changed is that two
addresses were assigned on a lane that already existed.

## What the "R" in 407R Arlington actually means

Three rear addresses arrived in April, and the honest answer about them is split in two.

The **specific** cases are documented. 407 Arlington Avenue has building permit
`25 104277 BLD`, structure type **"Laneway / Rear Yard Suite"**, issued 12 May 2025 —
alongside a demolition of the original house and a new three-storey fourplex. 623
Broadview Avenue has `25 222205 BLD`, described as a **garden suite**, issued 19 January
2026, with a matching Committee of Adjustment application for "a new two-storey detached
ancillary building (containing one garden suite …) in the rear yard".

The **general** claim is not. Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 598, *Numbering of
Properties*, contains no occurrence of the words "rear", "suffix", "letter" or "alpha".
The City's *Municipal Numbering of a Property* page lists what triggers a new address but
defines no suffix. Both the Laneway Suites and Garden Suites program pages say nothing
about addressing at all.

So: two of these three are laneway or garden suites, on the City's own permit records.
That the `R` suffix is *how Toronto numbers rear suites* is an inference, and nothing
published says it. My offline draft of this month asserted the direction from geometry
and happened to be right, which is not the same as having known.

The third, **885R Caledonia Road**, has no public-record explanation whatsoever — zero
building permits, zero committee applications, zero development applications. It exists,
and the record is silent.

Chapter 598 does explain one thing the file does daily, though. Section 598-2 names the
"One Address Repository" as "the official record of the municipal addresses of properties
within the City" — that is the dataset this newsletter reads — and requires every address
to carry one of "former Toronto," "East York," "York," "North York," "Etobicoke" or
"Scarborough". That is why a house in 2026 still says which city it was in before 1998.

## Two severances, one reissue

April's splits both trace to a Committee of Adjustment consent:

- **152 Pinegrove Avenue** → 152A and 152B. Consent `B0037/25SC`, approved 11 February
  2026: "to sever the property into two residential lots. The existing one-storey
  detached dwelling would be demolished," with two variances for a new three-storey
  triplex.
- **155 Poyntz Avenue** → 155A and 155B. Consent `B0002/25NY`, approved 14 August 2025,
  with variances for two new detached dwellings.

And **Bazalgette Drive** — where 29, 30 and 32 came off on 2 April and 27, 29, 30, 32, 34
all went on the next day under new record identifiers — has nothing behind it. No
development application, no committee file, no building permit, anywhere in the City's
data. That absence is itself the answer: it looks like record-keeping, not construction.

### How this is measured

Every day a snapshot of Toronto's Address Points dataset is pulled and diffed against the
one before it. This piece covers the stretch between the snapshots of **27 March 2026**
and **30 April 2026**. Counts are **net**: an address added and retired within the month
nets to nothing. Coordinate-only changes under 50 metres are dropped as noise; four such
edits on 24 April were held on those grounds.

Causes come from the City's own published records, linked below. Where no record exists —
885R Caledonia, Bazalgette Drive, the physical demolition date on Secord — this piece says
so rather than guessing. A permit is a licence to demolish, not proof of when a building
came down.
