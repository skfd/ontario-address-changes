# A street for Chloe Cooley, and a small error the City inherited

On 26 May 2026, six addresses appeared on a street that had never been in Toronto's
address file: 18, 20, 30, 32, 34 and 38 **Chloe Cooley Street**. All six arrived as
*reserved* — assigned, not yet in service. Nobody lives there. The street runs between
Richardson Street and Lower Sherbourne Street, north of Queens Quay East, on the East
Waterfront.

It has a by-law. **By-law 182-2026**, enacted 22 February 2026, authorises the naming of a
proposed public street at 215 Lake Shore Boulevard East as Chloe Cooley Street. The
decision was taken at Toronto and East York Community Council on 19 February — community
councils hold delegated authority over street naming, so it never reached full Council —
on a motion by Deputy Mayor Ausma Malik. The developer's application had been finalised
on 15 December 2025. Signage: about $600.

The addresses arrive three months and four days after the by-law.

## The report gets the enslaver's name wrong

Chloe Cooley was a Black woman enslaved in Queenston, Upper Canada. On **14 March 1793**
she was tied with rope, forced into a boat and taken across the Niagara River to be sold
in New York State. She resisted and screamed. Two men — Peter Martin, a Black Loyalist,
and William Grisley, a white labourer — reported it to the Executive Council of Upper
Canada a week later.

The City's naming report names her enslaver **William Vrooman**. Current scholarship
doesn't. The Canadian Encyclopedia (in an article by Natasha Henry-Dixon), the Dictionary
of Canadian Biography and Parks Canada all name **Adam Vrooman**, a Loyalist of Queenston.
The "William" appears to come from the Ontario Heritage Trust's 2007 plaque background
paper, which the report follows.

It is a small thing and it is now attached to a street. If you write about this street,
use Adam.

Two other things worth getting right, because both are commonly got wrong:

- **Vrooman was never punished.** Attorney General John White was directed to prosecute
  him for disturbing the peace. Vrooman petitioned that he had no idea it was illegal to
  sell his own property. The charge was dropped: enslaved people were chattel and had no
  legal standing.
- **The 1793 Act freed nobody.** *An Act to Prevent the further Introduction of Slaves*
  received Royal Assent on 9 July 1793. It banned importing enslaved people into Upper
  Canada, and — in the Canadian Encyclopedia's words — "at the outset, it confirmed and
  validated the institution of slavery." It did not outlaw selling enslaved people within
  the province, or across the border. Children born to enslaved mothers were to be freed
  at twenty-five. Slavery in the British Empire ended in 1834.

What became of Chloe Cooley after 14 March 1793 is unknown. The Dictionary of Canadian
Biography says so in one sentence: "Nothing is known of what became of Cooley afterwards."

The naming report notes that the City's own street-naming policy normally requires the
consent of the named party or a representative. Deputy Mayor Malik consulted the historian
Rochelle Bush, who confirmed that despite years of research **no descendants have been
found**. The naming proceeded on the basis that reasonable efforts had been made.

The naming passed without a single news story. I searched for one and there is none.

## The file called it Toronto Stadium before kickoff

On 12 May the place name at **170 Princes' Boulevard** changed from *CNE BMO Field* to
*Toronto Stadium, BMO Field*.

BMO Field hosted six 2026 World Cup matches — five group-stage games on 12, 17, 20, 23 and
26 June, and a Round of 32 match on 2 July — under the name **Toronto Stadium**, because
FIFA prohibits venue names carrying sponsors unaffiliated with the tournament. The same
thing happened for the 2014 U-20 Women's World Cup, when it was the National Soccer
Stadium.

What is nice about the file's version is that it kept both names, in exactly the form the
City itself was using: the City's own news release writes it "Toronto Stadium (BMO
Field)". A municipal address database picked up a temporary marketing constraint a month
before the first match, and preserved the permanent name alongside it.

## A park named in 2004, filed in 2026

Among May's place-name changes: *Rouge Community Park* became **Irma Burman Greenbelt
Park**.

That is not a 2026 renaming. Toronto City Council named it in **2004**, on a report from
the Commissioner of Economic Development, Culture and Tourism. Irma Burman was born on a
farm in Thorold in 1917, won the Irish Sweepstakes in 1955 and put the $2,000 toward a
down payment on the family's first house, and spent the sixties, seventies and eighties
working on the preservation of the Rouge Valley, Meadowvale Pond and Centennial Swamp.
The report credits her as "instrumental in the preservation of the greenbelt situated west
of Tallpines Court."

"Rouge Community Park" appears in no City record at all. The address file spent
twenty-one years behind on this one, and what looks like a renaming is a database catching
up with a decision older than most of the neighbourhood.

## One severance, one day, and one silence

Four addresses came off **Freeland Street** on 7 May — 15, 33, 43 and 53 — on the former
LCBO headquarters lands off Queens Quay East, now Sugar Wharf. A Committee of Adjustment
consent to sever that block into four lots was filed on **6 May 2026**, the day before.
That is a tight enough coincidence to report and too tight to prove; the severance record
sits on a JavaScript-only City page and should be confirmed in a browser before anyone
leans on it.

Three addresses came off **Polson Street** the same day — 1, 3 and 5, in the Port Lands.
Here the record is simply empty: no occupant, no development application, no building
permit, no demolition record. There are **zero** Polson Street records in the City's
development-applications dataset citywide. The Port Lands are under the largest
flood-protection and redevelopment project in the city, and it would be easy to write a
sentence connecting the two. Nothing supports it, so there isn't one.

## Two clusters, two permits, and one of them isn't housing

Both of May's reserved clusters have a building permit issued inside the same month:

- **Markham Road 2250–2288** (five addresses, 22 May) — permit `25 172298 BLD`, issued 8
  May. It is **self-storage, warehouse and retail**, not housing. Worth saying, because
  five new addresses in Scarborough North reads like homes and isn't.
- **Kingston Road 4630–4636** (four addresses, 7 May) — permit `25 125575 BLD`, issued 21
  May. This one is housing: "Everston", thirteen storeys, 566 rental units, settled at the
  Ontario Land Tribunal in 2024.

And on 22 May, **102, 102R, 104 and 104R Guestville Avenue** arrived together — two lots,
each with a front and a rear address assigned on the same day rather than years apart.
April's three rear addresses were retrofitted onto lots that already had numbers; these
four came as a matched set.

May's three splits were the usual shape — 144 Bexhill Avenue, 33 Bexhill Avenue and 212
Alderbrae Avenue each becoming A and B, originals still on file.

## A footnote for March's readers

March's article described eight renumberings that turned out to be the published file
reverting to values from a year earlier. One came back early: on 28 May, **151 Hudson
Drive** became **157 Hudson Drive** again. The other seven waited until 9 July.

### How this is measured

Every day a snapshot of Toronto's Address Points dataset is pulled and diffed against the
one before it. This piece covers the stretch between the snapshots of **30 April 2026** and
**29 May 2026**. Counts are **net**. Coordinate-only changes under 50 metres are dropped as
noise. May had no held events.
