Nine addresses that are really a procurement document

On 28 July 2026, nine addresses appeared in Toronto's file describing a subway line that isn't open. Two station entrances at Jane, two at Scarlett, a traction power substation, and four emergency exit buildings numbered EEB1, EEB4, EEB5 and EEB6.

Read Infrastructure Ontario's scope of work for the Eglinton Crosstown West Extension Stations, Rail and Systems contract and you find the same list, in the same units:

"Six emergency exit buildings." / "One standalone traction power sub-station."

The winning consortium's own release says it again — "six emergency exit buildings and a new traction power substation." Metrolinx's station pages confirm the rest: two entrances each at Jane-Eglinton and Scarlett, exactly the main-and-secondary pairs the file records.

So the file's numbering is not the City's. It is the contract's inventory, arriving in the municipal address database as civic addresses. Somebody had to give a door a number so that a permit could be issued, and the numbers they used are the ones on the drawings.

EEB2 and EEB3 are missing from the batch. They were built earlier under the advance-tunnel contract, which is a plausible reason and not a sourced one.

What the acronyms mean, and who says so

A traction power substation — the City of Toronto's own Ontario Line construction update spells it out — "is an electrical facility required to power the future electric […] trains." Metrolinx describes what one does: it takes "alternating current power that's available from normal power companies and converts it into the direct current required by the light rail vehicles."

An emergency exit building is the small structure that surfaces periodically along a tunnel; Metrolinx's plain description is that "the EEB will allow for safe exit from the tunnels." A widely-repeated claim that they must sit every 760 metres could not be sourced anywhere, so it isn't here.

And Matheson Boulevard is not a new street

The tempting reading of 20 Matheson Boulevard is that a street was brought into existence to hold an emergency exit. It is too good to be true.

Matheson Boulevard East is a real Toronto street: the short Etobicoke stub of the Mississauga arterial, by the Highway 427 and Eglinton Avenue West interchange, in exactly the ward the file assigns. What is new is its first address.

And the City had already prepared the ground. By-law 375-2024, passed in April 2024, rezoned a "portion of unaddressed lands at Eglinton Avenue West and Matheson Boulevard East to facilitate the Eglinton Crosstown West LRT Extension." Twenty-seven months later, those unaddressed lands got a number.

The file came back from 2025

On 17 March the published file reverted a set of fields to values it had last carried in our April 2025 baseline. On 9 July all of it returned in a single snapshot: 112 addresses on Mc Caul Street became McCaul Street again, eight Kimbark Boulevard houses went back to North York, seven of the day's nine renumberings exactly reversed March's, and 480 place-name edits landed at once.

One hundred and fourteen days. Nothing in the file said so.

The park renamings are not renamings — twice over

This is where the two halves of this newsletter collide, and it is worth being careful.

July's brief lists a batch of park renamings: Bain Avenue Parkette → Erica Stark Parkette, River Square Park → Lawren Harris Square, Keelesdale North Park → North Keelesdale Park, Beechgrove Park → Beechgrove Ravine.

None of them happened in July. All four names were already in the file by 9 September 2025; the March regression stripped them, and 9 July put them back. They are restorations.

And the real decisions are older still. Erica Stark Parkette was renamed by a 2016 community council decision, on a staff report responding to a request made on 15 June 2015. Erica Stark was a volunteer with the Friends of Withrow Park who "worked closely with the local Councillor to make improvements to the parkette, including advocating for better lighting, new playground equipment and the replacement of the gate at the south park entrance". In November 2014 she was struck and killed by a minivan that mounted the curb at Midland Avenue and Gilder Road, while walking her dog. She was the mother of three boys. The dedication ceremony was in June 2016.

So the address file recorded that name in 2025, lost it to a data regression in March 2026, and got it back in July — ten years after the decision, and twelve after her death. Nothing about July is the news here. The lag is.

Lawren Harris Square's naming decision could not be found at all, which is its own small surprise for a square named after a Group of Seven painter.

One block, two street names

Two of July's clusters looked separate and are the same place. Old Foundry Road (153, 163, 165, 175) and Palace Street (46, 48, 50, 52) are the north and south frontages of one site in the West Don Lands — the Dominion Foundry lands, where a heritage report describes the block as bounded "by Rolling Mills Road to the west, Old Foundry Road to the north, and Palace Street to the south," with three new residential buildings going up around the retained heritage structures, including a 31-storey affordable housing tower above the Machine Shop.

Old Foundry Road exists because a 2025 report renamed a section of Eastern Avenue. Exactly four Old Foundry points appear in the file, matching the four sites the renaming report says were addressed off that stretch.

The same collapsing happens downtown: Wellesley Street West 6–16 and Yonge Street 586/586A read as two unrelated sets of retirements and are one land assembly.

Coneflower Crescent deserves its own line. Thirty-seven addresses there flipped from reserved to regular on 22 July, having sat on the file since our first snapshot. It is Bloom Park Towns, off Bathurst in Westminster-Branson, on a plan of subdivision approved in 2013 and described as "newly constructed" in a 2017 City report. Thirteen years from subdivision to the file calling those addresses current.

One thing not to write

Cherry Street and Cooperage Street each lost two addresses in July, and both sit in the Port Lands, where Toronto is running its largest flood-protection and redevelopment project. It would be easy, and wrong, to connect them: the shape of the change looks like an alias collapse in the records rather than anything physical, and nothing ties it to the Port Lands work or to the Cherry Street realignment. No source, no sentence.

Also unfound: any street address for the emergency exit buildings or the substation outside this file — it may be their first public appearance anywhere; a project at Palace Street 46–52; "Beechgrove Ravine" in any City record; and a naming item for Coneflower Crescent.

How this is measured

Every day a snapshot of Toronto's Address Points dataset is pulled and diffed against the one before it. This piece covers the stretch between the snapshots of 30 June 2026 and 31 July 2026. Counts are net, and where an address was added and retired within the month we have said so. Coordinate-only changes under 50 metres are dropped as noise; 83 such edits on 9 July and 3 on 24 July were held on those grounds.

Causes come from published records, linked below. The claim that a document predates a file change is the load-bearing one throughout this piece, and every date in the lag table comes from a source, not from the address data.
